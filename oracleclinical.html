01 Keeping Physicians Patient-Focused

I worked closely with our physician subject matter experts (shoutout Dr Wall) to understand how documentation impacts them, and to most physicians?

Documentation is an intrusion, a frustration, and a distraction from what physicians really care about: their patients.

For every 8 hours doctors spend with patients, they spend 5 hours struggling through unusable EHR software to record long, bloated, redundant notes, a task significantly associated with physician burnout. [1] [2] [3]

It weaves its way into snatches of time wherever they can - glancing between their patient and the screen, quickly dictating in the hallway between visits, or in long hours tacked onto the end of a shift. Besides the practical difficulty of this task, doctors hate that they’re forced to spend less face-to-face time with patients.

How might we make clinical notes a simple background task, and let clinicians focus on their patient?

Hands-off and automatic documentation was the goal. Every minute saved writing was another minute of care and attention to their patient. So, let's first automate everything we can:

Then, ambient listening during a visit can fill in many of the gaps.

02 Capturing the Internal

But, automatically writing a note won't solve everything.

Notes aren't just describing what happened in a visit: they're a collection of information linked by the physician's medical decision-making.

Every data point that contributes to their assessment, every differential diagnoses they rule out in their heads, and the reasoning behind the plan they prescribe must be laid out for a legal, regulatory, and future medical trail.

Physicians navigate through the patient's record, gathering lab result, trends in vitals, previous visit notes, etc. and adding them to the note. "Copy-pasting" is a major legal no-no in medicine, so we created a shortcut in our software to collect this information with clear attribution and hyperlinks back to the original source.

03 Raising Quality Bar

Another learning from my physician partner is that since the transition to electronic health records, physician notes have gotten worse, not better. For repeat visits or long-term treatments, it became common practice to duplicate the old note and write more on top. EHR notes have grown 60% longer and 11% more redundant over the last few years. [3]

I saw an opportunity to improve the quality of note outcomes, not just the experience of making them, by integrating Oracle’s biggest differentiator - our AI capabilities.

04 Translating to Nursing

As the experience definition phase of documentation wrapped up with a roadshow of my final demos across the VP level, I continued to work with developers throughout implementation.

At the same time, the nursing product team reached out - they were starting a redesign of forms-based charting, and asked for me specifically based on my reputation with the other documentation PMs! :D So I took on a new project, building a great collaboration with our nursing experts (shoutout Tara and Anne!) and revolutionizing the charting experience.

Forms-based charting shares goals with notes; recording accurate, thorough information without breaking a clinician's attention to their patient. But, it's much more discrete data and many form fields, rather than written narrative. So audio capture was still a huge innovation, but quick keyboard macros and navigation were a unique value add.
